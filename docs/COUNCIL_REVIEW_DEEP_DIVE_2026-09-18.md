# Council review: an AI Daily deep-dive section

**Date:** 2026-09-18  
**Status:** research and operating recommendation; no public product change authorized  
**Scope:** a recurring section in which the most important or most discussed story receives a deeper, evidence-led treatment with an explicitly labeled AI Daily point of view and carefully sourced subject-matter perspectives.

## Executive recommendation

**Yes, add it — but as a selective editorial module, not a second daily publication and not a social-media popularity contest.**

The best first version is a **single text-first Deep Dive per edition**, normally 900–1,500 words, attached to one existing story. It should be publishable as a standalone article, readable in five to seven minutes, and optionally adaptable into a later audio script. It should not require an interview, an X post, or a new illustration to exist.

The deep dive should have four clearly separated layers:

1. **The record:** what primary documents and independently reported evidence establish.
2. **The interpretation:** what the evidence plausibly means, including the strongest competing interpretation where one exists.
3. **The point of view:** AI Daily’s bounded editorial judgment, signed as analysis and never presented as a fact.
4. **The open questions:** what would change the judgment and what readers should watch next.

X/Twitter and similar platforms should be treated as **lead and perspective sources**, not as a substitute for evidence. A relevant expert’s post may explain, challenge, or contextualize a claim. It does not independently verify the underlying event unless the expert has direct, disclosed knowledge and the claim is corroborated.

## Why the idea is valuable

A daily signal list answers “what moved?” A deep dive can answer “what should I believe, why, and what would change my mind?” This creates a useful editorial distinction without changing AI Daily into a chat show or unmoderated debate.

The case for the feature:

- It gives the most consequential story enough room for context that a card cannot provide.
- It makes AI Daily’s editorial judgment visible instead of smuggling opinion into supposedly neutral summaries.
- It creates a durable place for methodological explanation, numbers, timelines, and expert disagreement.
- It can connect the story to the existing Evidence Desk, Change Ledger, One Consequential Number, and reader experiment.
- It provides a future audio spine without making audio a dependency.

The risks:

- “Most talked about” can reward outrage, coordinated posting, or platform incentives instead of importance.
- Point of view can become unsupported AI commentary or advocacy disguised as analysis.
- Social posts are easy to misattribute, delete, quote out of context, or mistake for expertise.
- A multi-voice treatment can manufacture a false balance where the evidence is not contested.
- A daily long-form feature can exhaust editors and weaken the core briefing.
- Embedding or reproducing social content introduces privacy, copyright, accessibility, platform-policy, and permanence problems.

The recommendation is therefore **high value, conditional on a strict selection gate and a small pilot**.

## Research reviewed

### Podcast and deep-dive format research

The research points to a repeatable structure rather than a free-form conversation:

- Briefing-podcast guidance commonly separates a short opening, a few main stories with context and implications, and quick hits. The useful lesson is to give the deep dive a bounded run time and a clear promise rather than allowing it to swallow the daily edition.
- Descriptions of the AI Daily Brief show a useful model: rapid synthesis for the daily feed, with longer episodes for broad implications, practical use, or interviews. AI Daily should borrow the distinction between briefing and analysis, not imitate a host’s voice or production style.
- Research and production guidance for investigative and narrative podcasts repeatedly emphasizes a story spine, prepared questions, structured acts, clear transitions, and evidence notes. The strongest transferable idea is **over-prepare, then simplify**.
- Research on AI-generated podcast dialogue cautions that two speakers with near-equal turns can sound conversational while still being synthetic. A “panel of agents” should therefore stay backstage. The public article should have one accountable editorial voice; any audio version should use at most a host and a clearly identified guest or narrator.

### Journalism, social sourcing, and verification research

The council relied primarily on professional standards and research rather than social-media growth advice:

- Reuters guidance says named sources are preferable, social posts require identity and position-to-know checks, social sourcing must be attributed, and an editor should review an individual social-media source before publication. It also says not to submit finished copy to sources for approval; checking a quote or factual detail is different from giving a source editorial control.
- The SPJ guidance emphasizes that journalists remain responsible for accuracy, must verify information, identify sources and motives, provide context, and recognize the risks of using uncertain social-media material. It distinguishes legal access from ethical justification to publish.
- Research on Twitter/X sourcing finds that journalists often use social media for leads and statements but commonly verify outside the platform, prefer institutional or established sources, and use posts as an additional source rather than the sole basis of a claim.
- Verification guidance emphasizes provenance, source identity, date, location, direct contact where possible, and triangulation. Repetition or virality is not verification.
- Narrative-podcast guidance supports a cold open or clear question, a story spine, staged context, a central tension, and an ending that states what remains unresolved. These are presentation choices, not permission to withhold material facts for artificial suspense.

## Multi-persona council debate

The council used independent lenses before attempting consensus. Personas are editorial roles, not public “AI experts” and not published quotes.

### 1. Mara — managing editor

**Argument for:** The briefing needs a place where the editorial team can say what it thinks and show its work. A deep dive can become the edition’s durable anchor.

**Challenge:** “Most talked about” is not an editorial criterion by itself. A story can trend because it is emotionally contagious, astroturfed, or easy to argue about.

**Guidance:** Select by a two-axis test: public consequence and unresolved reader need. Use conversation volume only as a discovery signal. Require a named thesis question before commissioning.

### 2. Priya — evidence editor and fact-checker

**Argument for:** A deep dive can make claim-level evidence more legible than a short card.

**Challenge:** The longer the prose, the more opportunities there are for an inference to drift into fact. Social posts often collapse observation, prediction, and advocacy into one paragraph.

**Guidance:** Build a claim ledger before drafting. Label each sentence or paragraph as fact, source-reported claim, inference, opinion, or open question. Every material factual claim must point to a source record. No AI-generated or reconstructed quote.

### 3. Tomas — subject-matter editor

**Argument for:** Experts can explain mechanisms and trade-offs that primary announcements omit.

**Challenge:** The most visible experts may have commercial, ideological, or personal incentives. A credential is not proof that the person knows this specific question.

**Guidance:** Match expertise to the claim, not the brand. Record domain, role, relevant work, conflicts, and whether the person has direct knowledge. Seek at least one practitioner or affected stakeholder when the story is about deployment, not only researchers and executives.

### 4. Leila — social verification editor

**Argument for:** X, Bluesky, LinkedIn, GitHub discussions, and specialist forums can expose rapid technical interpretation, corrections, and first-hand context before formal articles exist.

**Challenge:** Verification badges, follower counts, repost volume, and confident language are weak proxies for reliability. Screenshots are especially fragile.

**Guidance:** Use social platforms in three separate ways: lead, direct statement, and expert interpretation. Record the exact post URL, author identity, timestamp retrieved, whether the post is original or quoting someone else, and what was independently checked. Link to the canonical post where possible; do not rely on a screenshot as the only record.

### 5. Noor — public-interest and harm editor

**Argument for:** A careful deep dive can slow down sensational safety stories and make affected people visible.

**Challenge:** Deep dives can turn incidents, vulnerable people, or harmful capabilities into entertainment. Repeating operational details can increase harm.

**Guidance:** Apply the existing non-operational safety policy. Minimize identifying details about private people. Do not turn a disagreement into a “both sides” segment if one side has no credible evidence. Add a harm review for cyber, bio, mental health, minors, and active incidents.

### 6. Erik — product and accessibility editor

**Argument for:** A labeled module can improve navigation, depth, and reader trust while remaining optional.

**Challenge:** A podcast-inspired treatment can become audio-first, visual-first, or dependent on social embeds. That would break the canonical read mode.

**Guidance:** The canonical artifact is a static article with headings, plain text, data tables or captions, source links, and a transcript if audio exists. Audio, social cards, diagrams, and embeds are progressive enhancements. No key claim should exist only in an iframe, image, or audio clip.

### 7. Sacha — audio producer

**Argument for:** The deep-dive structure is a strong future script: hook, record, tension, expert perspective, judgment, unknowns.

**Challenge:** A synthetic roundtable of multiple personas would sound authoritative while hiding who is accountable. It also risks flattening genuine disagreement into scripted banter.

**Guidance:** Pilot text first. If audio follows, use a single accountable narrator or a real, consented guest. Keep a full text transcript and source list. Do not fabricate an expert voice or imply that a sourced social post is an interview.

### 8. Arjun — legal, rights, and platform-policy editor

**Argument for:** Direct links and short, attributed quotations can make the sourcing trail inspectable.

**Challenge:** Posts can be deleted or edited; copying long posts or images may create rights and privacy issues; platform embeds can fail, track readers, or change without notice.

**Guidance:** Prefer links, short quotes, and paraphrase with attribution. Capture retrieval metadata internally. Do not archive or republish deleted material by default. Get permission for extended excerpts, recordings, or direct interviews. Provide a static source note when an embed is unavailable.

### 9. Ada — skeptical audience editor

**Argument for:** Readers often want to know what a development means, not only that it happened.

**Challenge:** “AI Daily’s view” can be perceived as an institutional conclusion with more confidence than the evidence supports.

**Guidance:** Make the opinion small, explicit, falsifiable, and revisable: “Our read, based on the record available on [date], is…” Then state what evidence would change it. Invite corrections to evidence, not performative voting on facts.

## Debate outcomes

### Question: Should “most talked about” choose the deep dive?

**Decision:** No. Use a composite selection score:

- 35% public consequence or decision impact.
- 25% unresolved reader question or evidence gap.
- 20% source quality and inspectability.
- 10% distinctiveness from yesterday’s coverage.
- 10% public attention or reader demand.

Attention is a tie-breaker or discovery input, not a truth or importance score.

### Question: Should each story include opposing experts?

**Decision:** No forced symmetry. Seek a meaningful alternative interpretation, affected stakeholder, or methodological critique where one exists. Say plainly when the disagreement is over values, forecasts, or evidence quality rather than pretending there are two equally supported factual accounts.

### Question: Can X/Twitter experts be used?

**Decision:** Yes, under a higher bar than casual citation. Social posts may supply leads, first-hand observations, interpretation, or a public response. They cannot stand alone for a consequential factual claim unless the person is directly positioned to know and the claim is independently checked or transparently qualified. A verified badge is identity evidence at most, not expertise or truth evidence.

### Question: Should the feature be generated daily?

**Decision:** No. Commission it only when the selection gate passes. A “no deep dive today” state is healthier than a padded feature. Start with two or three pilot editions per week or one per edition only when the evidence and editorial question justify it.

### Question: Should the deep dive launch with audio?

**Decision:** No. Launch text-first. Add audio only after the text, source ledger, and correction path work reliably. A transcript is mandatory if audio is published.

### Question: Should AI personas appear in the public article?

**Decision:** No. Personas remain a backstage review method. The article should name real sources and the accountable AI Daily editorial voice, not present simulated debate as expert opinion.

## Recommended editorial format

### Public module title

**DEEP DIVE · [specific question]**

Example:

> DEEP DIVE · What changed when a safety evaluation crossed a real boundary?

Avoid “The truth about…” and other claims of finality.

### Target length

- 900–1,500 words for the standard web edition.
- 600–900 words for a constrained day.
- 1,500–2,200 words only when the evidence file justifies it and an editor approves the exception.
- Optional audio adaptation: approximately 6–10 minutes, never a requirement for publication.

### Suggested structure

1. **The question** — one sentence explaining why this story deserves depth.
2. **The short answer** — two or three sentences with the current editorial read.
3. **What the record says** — timeline, primary documents, numbers, and what is directly established.
4. **What changed** — explicit delta from the prior edition or prior public record.
5. **The strongest interpretation** — the most defensible reading of the evidence.
6. **The pressure test** — the best-supported objection, limitation, or competing explanation.
7. **Expert lens** — two to four relevant perspectives, clearly labeled as analysis, not evidence of the event.
8. **AI Daily’s point of view** — a bounded, attributed editorial judgment with confidence and reasoning.
9. **What would change our mind** — specific future evidence or milestone.
10. **Watch next** — one to three concrete signals.
11. **Evidence Desk** — claim ledger, source tiers, retrieval dates, social links, conflicts, and corrections path.

### Perspective labels

Use visible labels:

- **Primary record**
- **Independent reporting**
- **Expert analysis**
- **Company or stakeholder position**
- **AI Daily analysis**
- **Open question**

Do not call an X post “expert evidence.” Call it “public analysis by [name/role]” or “first-hand account,” only after verification.

## Source and expert protocol

### Candidate discovery

The scout may search X/Twitter, Bluesky, LinkedIn, GitHub, Reddit, research blogs, conference talks, and podcasts to find people, arguments, and leads. Discovery results stay in the candidate packet.

### Expert qualification record

For every proposed expert, store:

- Display name and canonical profile URL.
- Affiliation and relevant role at the time of posting.
- Relevant work, paper, project, or direct experience.
- Relationship to the subject and financial or institutional conflicts.
- Whether the post is original analysis, quotation, prediction, marketing, or first-hand observation.
- Exact post URL, post timestamp, retrieval timestamp, and edit/deletion status if observable.
- Permission status for extended quotation, recording, or direct interview.
- Which claim or question the expert is qualified to address.

### Verification rules

1. Start with the primary document, data, release, paper, filing, or direct record.
2. Use social posts to generate questions or add interpretation.
3. Confirm identity and relevant expertise outside the post where possible.
4. Contact the person directly for consequential first-hand claims or quotations.
5. Independently verify material factual claims.
6. Attribute the source precisely and disclose uncertainty.
7. Use short quotations; prefer paraphrase with a link.
8. Do not quote deleted or private posts without an exceptional, documented public-interest reason and editor approval.
9. Do not use follower count, verification badge, repost count, or virality as expertise evidence.
10. Preserve a private research snapshot and retrieval metadata, but do not automatically republish screenshots or personal data.

## Integration plan

### Phase 1 — text-first pilot

- Add `deep_dive` to the additive contract and TypeScript types.
- Add `DeepDive.tsx` as an optional module, rendered only when the approved edition contains it.
- Place it after the edition contents and before the full story list, with a visible “Deep Dive” heading and a linked story anchor.
- Add a compact “Why this story” selection note and evidence posture.
- Render sections as real headings and paragraphs; use a table only for genuinely tabular data.
- Render expert perspectives as labeled source cards with profile, post, evidence, conflict, and retrieval metadata. Do not rely on social embeds.
- Add a static fallback when a post is deleted, unavailable, or blocked.
- Link every factual section back to the Evidence Desk and claim IDs.
- Add a correction and “what would change our mind” block.
- Keep the module optional and text-first; no audio generation yet.

### Phase 2 — review workflow

- Add a deep-dive candidate packet under `data/candidates/YYYY-MM-DD/`.
- Add dedicated council scores for consequence, evidence, expertise fit, independence, harm, and reader value.
- Add a deterministic gate: no public deep dive without all required fields, source links, expert verification notes, and editor approval.
- Add tests for missing source trails, unverified expert claims, absent point-of-view labels, and social-only factual claims.
- Add a “no deep dive today” outcome to the run packet.

### Phase 3 — audio adaptation

- Convert the approved text into a script, preserving the article as canonical.
- Use a single consistent narrator or a real, consented guest; do not synthesize a fake expert voice.
- Publish an accessible transcript and chapter markers.
- Preserve exact source links in the show notes.
- Keep audio generation behind the same approval gate; never auto-publish from the script draft.

### Phase 4 — reader feedback

- Add a lightweight correction and question route, not a public unmoderated debate.
- Track which claims readers challenge and route them to the Evidence Desk.
- Do not rank experts, turn disagreement into a poll, or use engagement as a proxy for truth.

## Acceptance checklist for each deep dive

### Selection

- [ ] It answers a consequential unresolved question.
- [ ] It is not selected solely because it is trending.
- [ ] It is materially distinct from the short story treatment.
- [ ] It does not repeat yesterday’s story without a real delta.

### Evidence

- [ ] A primary record is present where one exists.
- [ ] Every material factual claim has a claim ID and evidence URL.
- [ ] Company claims, preprints, expert opinions, and AI Daily analysis are visibly distinguished.
- [ ] Data includes units, dates, denominator, methodology, and limitations.
- [ ] Conflicting evidence is represented accurately; false balance is avoided.

### Experts and social platforms

- [ ] Each expert is relevant to the exact question.
- [ ] Identity, role, work, and conflicts are checked.
- [ ] Post URL and retrieval time are recorded.
- [ ] Social material is not the sole evidence for a consequential fact.
- [ ] Quotes are accurate, short, permission-aware, and not reconstructed.
- [ ] Deleted, private, or vulnerable-person content has not been casually republished.

### Point of view and harm

- [ ] AI Daily’s point of view is labeled as analysis.
- [ ] Confidence is stated.
- [ ] The article says what would change the view.
- [ ] Safety content is non-operational.
- [ ] The article does not imply that staged agent debate is real expert testimony.

### Product

- [ ] Read mode works without embeds, scripts, audio, or color.
- [ ] Source links are keyboard accessible and descriptive.
- [ ] The module has a static fallback for unavailable posts.
- [ ] Archive generation keeps the deep dive attached to the correct edition.
- [ ] Candidate and approved artifacts are separate.
- [ ] Human approval is recorded before publication.

## Final disposition

**Approve a text-first pilot in the existing additive architecture. Do not yet build a public social feed, autonomous expert panel, or daily audio pipeline.**

The feature should be called **Deep Dive**, should appear at most once per edition, and should be omitted when the selection or evidence gate fails. The Evidence Desk remains the canonical provenance layer; the deep dive is a richer editorial treatment built on top of it, not a replacement for it.
